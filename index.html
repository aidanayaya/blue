Research Question

Does the timing of redistricting — decennial versus mid-decade — predict the degree of partisan bias in U.S. congressional maps drawn between 2011 and 2026?

Independent Variable

Redistricting timing — binary. 0 = decennial (2011, 2021 cycles). 1 = mid-decade (2025–26 wave).

Dependent Variable

Partisan bias, measured as the absolute value of the efficiency gap — a percentage, signed toward either party (D or R) before conversion to absolute value. Theoretically unbounded; empirically observed from 0% up to roughly 20–25% in the most extreme real-world cases.

Why 2011

The first congressional maps drawn after the 2010 Census — the normal, on-schedule decennial cycle, and the earliest point where modern efficiency-gap data is reliably available.

Positioning

Positivist Interpretivist Descriptive

This project follows a positivist epistemology: it treats partisan bias as an objectively measurable outcome, tests a falsifiable, directional hypothesis derived deductively from theory before data collection, rather than allowing the question to evolve through fieldwork.

Contribution

Researchers have studied how redistricting institutions affect partisan bias, but they have not studied whether redistricting timing does too — a question that couldn't be tested until the 2025–26 mid-decade wave produced the first real cases.

The Gap

No peer-reviewed source yet tests redistricting timing against partisan bias outcomes using the 2025–26 mid-decade cases. This absence reflects publication timing, not a lack of scholarly interest — the phenomenon itself is new.

Literature Review

Consensus

McCartan, Kenny, Simko, Ebowe, Zhao & Imai (2026)
American Political Science Review

Diff-in-diff design across the 2010/2020 cycles; institutional constraints on partisan actors reduce bias, and constraint design matters.

"Independent Redistricting Commissions Are Associated with More Competitive Elections"
PS: Political Science & Politics

Peer-reviewed APSA journal; commissions correlate with more competitive districts.

Debate

"A Retrospective on Redistricting Practices and Electoral Competition in U.S. Elections" (2025)
State Politics & Policy Quarterly

Synthesizes the optimist/skeptic split on how much commission design actually matters.

McGhee (2014), "Measuring Partisan Bias in Single-Member District Electoral Systems"
Legislative Studies Quarterly

Refines how partisan bias should be measured; finds effects of party control on bias are small and decay rapidly — a genuinely contrary finding, live scholarly disagreement.

Theoretical Mechanism

Owen & Grofman (1988), "Optimal Partisan Gerrymandering"
Political Geography Quarterly

Foundational theory: uncertainty about future voter behavior constrains how aggressively a rational party optimizes a gerrymander.

Theory & Hypothesis

Because uncertainty about shifting political tides normally keeps gerrymanders moderate (Owen and Grofman 1988), and mid-decade redistricting reduces that uncertainty by letting a party optimize against more recently observed election results over a shorter risk horizon, mid-decade maps should show higher partisan bias than maps drawn on the regular decennial schedule.

Mid-decade timing → More recent election data → Less uncertainty → More aggressive optimization → Higher bias
+ |Partisan bias| ← + Mid-decade timing

Coded: 0 = decennial, 1 = mid-decade. DV = |efficiency gap|.

Limitations

  • Small treatment group. Roughly n=4–6 mid-decade cases exist, because voluntary mid-decade redistricting was almost nonexistent before 2025 — a direct consequence of the gap this project addresses, not a design flaw.
  • Projected, not observed, mid-decade scores. 2026 efficiency gap figures are model-based projections built from real historical votes applied to new district lines, since the 2026 election hasn't occurred yet.
  • Mixed case motivations. A few 2025–26 states redrew under court order rather than pure partisan discretion; these remain in the sample for simplicity, though the mechanism applies most cleanly to voluntary redraws.
